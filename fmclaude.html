1. The basics — what even is a fluid?

A fluid is anything that flows and takes the shape of its container — both liquids (water, oil) and gases (air) count. The one thing that makes fluid mechanics different from solid mechanics: fluids can't resist a shearing force while standing still — they just keep deforming (flowing) instead.

Think of pushing sideways on a brick (solid) vs pushing sideways on water in a bucket. The brick pushes back and stays put; the water just moves out of the way.

Density (ρ) and Specific Gravity (SG)

ρ = m / V   |   SG = ρ / ρwater   (ρwater = 1000 kg/m³)

Density just tells you how "packed" the mass is. Specific gravity is a shortcut — it compares a fluid to water, so it has no units.

Density of a gas (ideal gas law)

ρ = P / (R T)   [Rair = 0.287 kPa·m³/(kg·K), T in Kelvin]
Worked example (matches typical test phrasing): "A room is 4 m × 5 m × 6 m at 100 kPa and 25°C. Find the density, specific gravity, and mass of the air."
Step 1 — convert T to Kelvin: 25 + 273.15 = 298.15 K
Step 2 — plug into ρ = P/(RT): ρ = 100 / (0.287 × 298.15) = 1.17 kg/m³
Step 3 — SG = 1.17 / 1000 = 0.00117
Step 4 — Volume = 4×5×6 = 120 m³ → m = ρV = 1.17 × 120 = 140 kg
Test pattern to remember: "find density → SG → mass" is a very common 3-part question chain. Always do them in that order since each answer feeds the next.

2. Pressure & hydrostatics

Pressure is just force spread over an area (P = F/A). In a fluid at rest, pressure only depends on how deep you are — not on the shape of the container.

P = Patm + ρgh    (this is "gauge pressure" + atmospheric = "absolute pressure")
Gauge vs Absolute — the #1 mixed-up concept:
• Gauge pressure = ρgh only (measured relative to atmosphere, what a pressure gauge reads)
• Absolute pressure = Patm + ρgh (measured from a true vacuum)
If the question gives you Patm, it's almost always hinting that it wants the absolute answer too.
Worked example — Manometer: "A manometer fluid has SG = 0.85. The column height is 55 cm, and atmospheric pressure is 96 kPa. Find the gauge and absolute pressure."
Step 1 — ρ = SG × 1000 = 850 kg/m³
Step 2 — Gauge: P = ρgh = 850 × 9.81 × 0.55 = 4586 Pa = 4.6 kPa
Step 3 — Absolute: 96 + 4.6 = 100.6 kPa

Pascal's Law — hydraulic lifts

F₁ / A₁ = F₂ / A₂

Pressure applied anywhere in an enclosed fluid transmits equally everywhere. This is why a small force on a small piston can lift a huge weight on a big piston.

Worked example: "A hydraulic jack has a small piston of area 21 cm² and force 38 N. The large piston has area 100 cm². Find the output force."
F₂ = F₁ × (A₂/A₁) = 38 × (100/21) = 181 N

3. Forces on submerged surfaces & buoyancy

When a flat surface (a window, a dam wall, a gate) is submerged, water pushes on every point of it, but pressure increases with depth — so the push isn't uniform. We collapse all that push into one single equivalent force, FR, acting at one point, hp (the "centre of pressure").

FR = ρ g hc A    (hc = depth to the centroid of the shape)
hp = hc + Ic / (hc A)   (centre of pressure is always slightly below the centroid)
You'll need the second moment of area Ic for common shapes — for a circle, Ic = πD⁴/64; for a rectangle, Ic = bh³/12. These are usually given on a formula sheet, but memorising the circle one is worth it since it appears often.
Worked example: "A circular window of diameter 0.3 m has its centre 5 m deep in seawater (SG 1.025). Find the resultant force and its location."
Step 1 — hc = 5 m (already given as the centre)
Step 2 — A = πD²/4 = π(0.3)²/4 = 0.0707 m²
Step 3 — ρ = 1025 kg/m³ → FR = 1025 × 9.81 × 5 × 0.0707 ≈ 3550 N
Step 4 — Ic = πD⁴/64 = π(0.3)⁴/64 = 3.98×10⁻⁴ m⁴
Step 5 — hp = 5 + 3.98×10⁻⁴/(5×0.0707) ≈ 5.001 m
Notice hp barely moved from hc — that's normal for small shapes deep underwater. If a shape is close to the surface, the shift becomes much more noticeable, and a test may ask you to comment on why.

Buoyancy (Archimedes' principle)

FB = ρfluid g Vdisplaced

An object submerged (or floating) in a fluid feels an upward push equal to the weight of the fluid it displaces. If you're weighing something with a scale while it's submerged, the true weight and the "apparent" (measured) weight differ by exactly FB.

Worked example: "A steel sphere of diameter 1 m (ρ = 8050 kg/m³) hangs from a scale. Find the tension in air, then find the tension when fully submerged in water."
Step 1 — V = (4/3)π(0.5)³ = 0.5236 m³
Step 2 — Weight in air: W = ρsteel g V = 8050 × 9.81 × 0.5236 ≈ 41.35 kN
Step 3 — Buoyant force: FB = 1000 × 9.81 × 0.5236 ≈ 5.14 kN
Step 4 — Submerged tension: T = W − FB = 41.35 − 5.14 = 36.2 kN

4. Flow, continuity & Bernoulli's equation

Continuity is just "conservation of mass" for a moving fluid: whatever mass flows in one end of a pipe must flow out the other end (assuming nothing is stored inside).

ρ₁A₁V₁ = ρ₂A₂V₂    → for incompressible flow: A₁V₁ = A₂V₂
Squeeze a garden hose (reduce A) and the water shoots out faster (V increases) — that's continuity in action.

Bernoulli's equation (the "energy conservation" of fluid flow)

P/ρg + V²/2g + z = constant   (along a streamline, no losses, steady, incompressible)

This says: pressure energy + kinetic energy + elevation energy stays the same as a fluid particle moves along, as long as nothing is adding/removing energy or losing it to friction.

Worked example — Torricelli's theorem: "A large tank has an opening 5 m below the free surface. Find the exit velocity."
Apply Bernoulli between the free surface (V≈0, P=atm) and the exit (P=atm): ½V² = gh
V = √(2gh) = √(2 × 9.81 × 5) ≈ 9.9 m/s

Venturi meter — measuring flow rate

Q = A₁ / √((A₁/A₂)² − 1) × √(2gh(ρman/ρ − 1))
This formula looks intimidating, but it's just Bernoulli + continuity combined for a pipe that narrows. In an exam, plug in carefully and don't rush the square roots.
Worked example: "A venturi meter has d₁ = 250 mm, d₂ = 100 mm, carries oil (SG 0.9), with a mercury manometer reading 0.63 m. Find Q."
A₁ = π(0.25)²/4 = 0.0491 m², A₂ = π(0.10)²/4 = 0.00785 m²
Q = 0.0491/√((0.0491/0.00785)² − 1) × √(2×9.81×0.63×(13600/900 − 1)) ≈ 0.10 m³/s

Pitot-static tube — measuring air speed

V = √(2 ρwater g h / ρair)
Worked example: "A pitot tube in an airflow shows a manometer reading of h = 7.3 cm of water. Air density = 1.25 kg/m³. Find air velocity."
V = √(2 × 1000 × 9.81 × 0.073 / 1.25) ≈ 33.8 m/s

5. The momentum equation

Bernoulli is about energy; the momentum equation is about force. Whenever a fluid changes speed or direction (hits a plate, bends around an elbow, exits a jet), it takes a force to make that happen — and by Newton's 3rd law, the fluid pushes back with an equal and opposite force.

F = ṁ (Vout − Vin)    where ṁ = ρQ = ρAV
How to approach every momentum question:
1. Draw a control volume around the fluid.
2. Pick a positive direction (usually the initial flow direction).
3. List every force acting: pressure forces at inlet/outlet, and the unknown reaction force FR.
4. Apply ΣF = ṁ(Vout − Vin) in each direction separately (x and z).
Worked example — jet on a flat plate: "A water jet of diameter 50 mm and velocity 18 m/s hits a stationary flat plate perpendicularly. Find the force on the plate."
A = π(0.05)²/4 = 1.963×10⁻³ m²
F = ρAV² = 1000 × 1.963×10⁻³ × 18² ≈ 636 N
Worked example — 90° elbow: "Water flows at 25 kg/s through a 10 cm diameter pipe with a 90° bend. Gauge pressure at inlet is 3.43 kPa. Find the anchoring force needed to hold the elbow."
V = ṁ/(ρA) = 25/(1000 × π×0.1²/4) = 3.18 m/s
FRx = −ṁV − P₁A₁ (x-direction, flow decelerated to zero then redirected)
FRz = ṁV (z-direction, flow accelerated from zero)
Combine: FR = √(FRx² + FRz²) ≈ 133 N

6. Laminar vs turbulent flow & pipe losses

Laminar flow moves in smooth, orderly layers (like honey). Turbulent flow is chaotic, with swirling eddies (like fast river rapids). The Reynolds number tells you which one you have.

Re = ρVD/μ = VD/ν
Re valueFlow type
Re < 2300Laminar (smooth)
2300 < Re < 4000Transitional
Re > 4000Turbulent (chaotic)
Worked example: "Oil (ρ=860 kg/m³, μ=0.05 Pa·s) flows at 0.6 m/s through a 0.1 m diameter pipe. Is the flow laminar or turbulent?"
Re = 860 × 0.6 × 0.1 / 0.05 = 1032 → Laminar

Laminar pipe flow — Hagen-Poiseuille

Q = ΔP π D⁴ / (128 μ L)
Worked example: "Oil flows through a horizontal 5 cm diameter pipe, 40 m long, with pressure drop 648 kPa. μ = 0.8 Pa·s. Find Q."
Q = 648000 × π × (0.05)⁴ / (128 × 0.8 × 40) ≈ 0.00311 m³/s

Head loss in pipes — Darcy-Weisbach

hf = f (L/D)(V²/2g)
Worked example: "Water flows at 1 m/s through a 500 mm pipe, 1.5 km long, friction factor f = 0.02. Find the head loss."
hf = 0.02 × (1500/0.5) × (1²/(2×9.81)) ≈ 3.06 m

7. Water hammer

When a valve closes suddenly on a flowing pipe, the fluid can't stop instantly — this creates a pressure spike that travels back through the pipe as a shock wave. This is "water hammer," and the maths depends on whether the closure is fast or slow relative to how long it takes the pressure wave to travel down and back.

tc = 2L / C   (critical time — the round trip of the pressure wave)
Rapid closure (t < tc):   Δp = ρ C V
Slow closure (t > tc):   Δp = 2ρLV / t
Worked example: "A 50 cm diameter, 100 m long pipe carries 0.5 m³/s of water. Wave speed C = 1430 m/s. A valve closes in 1 second. Find the pressure rise."
V = Q/A = 0.5/(π×0.25²) ≈ 2.546 m/s
tc = 2×100/1430 = 0.14 s → since 1 s > 0.14 s, this is slow closure
Δp = 2×1000×100×2.546/1 ≈ 509 kPa
Always check t vs tc first — using the wrong formula (rapid instead of slow) is the most common mistake here, and it gives an answer many times too large.

8. Drag & boundary layers

When an object moves through a fluid (or fluid flows past it), the fluid resists that motion — this resistance is drag.

FD = CD A ρV² / 2

CD (drag coefficient) depends on shape — a flat plate has a different CD than a sphere or a car body. It's usually given in the question or a table.

Worked example: "A car has frontal area 2.31 m², travels at 25 m/s in air (ρ=1.164 kg/m³) and experiences 350 N of drag. Find CD."
CD = 2FD/(Aρ V²) = 2×350/(2.31×1.164×25²) ≈ 0.42

Flat-plate friction drag

Laminar: Cf = 1.33/√ReL   |   Turbulent: Cf = 0.074/ReL1/5

Terminal velocity of a settling sphere

V = √[ 4gD(ρs/ρf − 1) / (3CD) ]
Worked example: "A 4 mm sphere (ρ=1150 kg/m³) settles through water (ρ=998 kg/m³), CD=0.5. Find terminal velocity."
V = √[4×9.81×0.004×(1150/998 −1)/(3×0.5)] ≈ 0.126 m/s
"Terminal velocity" means the sphere has stopped accelerating — drag force exactly balances the net weight (weight minus buoyancy). That's the physical idea behind the formula.

🎯 Practice like the real test

Try these before looking at the answer. Each mirrors the style/numbers-flavor of the worked examples above.

Q1. A tank of glycerin (SG = 1.26) has a depth of 3 m. Atmospheric pressure is 101 kPa. Find the absolute pressure at the bottom.
ρ = 1260 kg/m³ → Pgauge = 1260×9.81×3 = 37.07 kPa → Pabs = 101 + 37.07 = 138.1 kPa
Q2. A rectangular gate 2 m wide × 1.5 m tall is vertical, with its top edge at the water surface. Find the resultant hydrostatic force.
hc = 1.5/2 = 0.75 m, A = 2×1.5 = 3 m² → FR = 1000×9.81×0.75×3 ≈ 22.1 kN
Q3. A wooden block (ρ=600 kg/m³) of volume 0.2 m³ floats in water. What fraction of its volume is submerged?
Floating → weight = buoyancy: ρwoodVg = ρwaterVsubg → Vsub/V = ρwood/ρwater = 600/1000 = 0.6 (60% submerged)
Q4. Water flows through a pipe that narrows from 20 cm to 10 cm diameter. If velocity in the wide section is 2 m/s, find the velocity in the narrow section.
A₁V₁=A₂V₂ → V₂ = V₁(D₁/D₂)² = 2×(20/10)² = 8 m/s
Q5. A jet of water (d = 40 mm, V = 20 m/s) strikes a stationary flat plate head-on. Find the force on the plate.
A = π(0.04)²/4 = 1.257×10⁻³ m² → F = ρAV² = 1000×1.257×10⁻³×20² = 503 N
Q6. Water (ν = 1×10⁻⁶ m²/s) flows at 0.3 m/s through a 40 mm diameter pipe. Determine whether the flow is laminar or turbulent.
Re = VD/ν = 0.3×0.04/1×10⁻⁶ = 12,000 → Turbulent (well above 4000)
Before the test, make sure you can:
✔ Convert units without hesitating (mm→m, cm²→m², L/s→m³/s)
✔ Tell gauge vs absolute pressure apart instantly
✔ Know when to use Bernoulli (no losses) vs when to add hf
✔ Draw a control volume before every momentum question
✔ Check Re before choosing laminar or turbulent formulas
✔ Check t vs tc before choosing the water hammer formula
