CH 2Pressure & Manometers

The single most reliable mark-earner on the paper. Master the "walk the tube" method.

Core formulas

Hydrostatic pressure
P = P₀ + ρgh
Density from SG
ρ = SG × 1000 kg/m³
Specific weight
γ = ρg  (N/m³)
Pressure head
h = P / (ρg)
Absolute pressure
Pabs = Pgage + Patm
Unit bridge
1 kPa = 1000 N/m²  ·  1 MPa = 10⁶ Pa

The manometer rule (learn this cold)

  1. Start at one end of the manometer (usually the unknown).
  2. Going DOWN in the fluid → ADD ρgh.
  3. Going UP → SUBTRACT ρgh.
  4. Same fluid at the same level → same pressure (this is why you can "jump" across a U-tube at the lower interface level).
  5. Set the result equal to the pressure at the other end. Solve.
Time-saver: write every term in kPa by dividing ρgh by 1000. Then you never touch scientific notation.

Worked Example A — Simple manometer (open to atmosphere)

A manometer measures the pressure in a tank containing a fluid of SG = 0.85. The column height is 55 cm and atmospheric pressure is 96 kPa. Find the absolute and gauge pressure in the tank.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Density of the manometer fluid
    ρ = SG × 1000 = 0.85 × 1000 = 850 kg/m³
  2. Gauge pressure (this is what the column measures)
    Pgage = ρgh = 850 × 9.81 × 0.55 = 4586 Pa = 4.59 kPa
  3. Absolute pressure
    Pabs = Patm + Pgage = 96 + 4.59 = 100.6 kPa
Pabs = 100.6 kPa  ·  Pgage = 4.6 kPa

Worked Example B — Multifluid manometer ⭐ (most likely exam question)

A tank is at an altitude where Patm = 85.6 kPa. The manometer has: h₁ = 0.1 m of water, h₂ = 0.2 m of oil (SG = 0.85), and h₃ = 0.35 m of mercury (SG = 13.6). Determine the air pressure in the tank.

Given: SGwater = 1.0 · SGoil = 0.85 · SGHg = 13.6

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Walk the tube from the air, going down/up to the open end:
    P₁ + ρwgh₁ + ρoilgh₂ − ρHggh₃ = Patm
  2. Rearrange for P₁:
    P₁ = Patm − ρwgh₁ − ρoilgh₂ + ρHggh₃
  3. Substitute densities (kg/m³) and heights:
    P₁ = 85.6 + 9.81 × [(13600)(0.35) − (1000)(0.1) − (850)(0.2)] / 1000
  4. Work the bracket:
    4760 − 100 − 170 = 4490
    P₁ = 85.6 + 9.81 × 4490 / 1000 = 85.6 + 44.05
Pair = 129.7 ≈ 130 kPa
Note the sign logic: mercury goes down (add), water and oil go up (subtract). Getting signs right is 80% of the marks here.

Worked Example C — Pressure expressed as head

A gauge pressure is 350 kN/m². Express it as a head of (a) water, (b) mercury (RD = 13.6). Also find the absolute pressure if Patm = 101.3 kN/m².

Show step-by-step solution
  1. (a) Water:
    h = P/(ρg) = 350 × 10³ / (1000 × 9.81) = 35.68 m
  2. (b) Mercury:
    h = 350 × 10³ / (13.6 × 1000 × 9.81) = 2.62 m
  3. Absolute pressure:
    Pabs = 350 + 101.3 = 451.3 kN/m²
(a) 35.68 m H₂O  ·  (b) 2.62 m Hg  ·  Pabs = 451.3 kN/m²

Quick drill — Diver pressure

A diver descends 30 m in seawater (ρ = 1025 kg/m³). Find the increase in pressure.

Show answer

ΔP = ρgΔh = 1025 × 9.81 × 30 = 301,700 Pa

ΔP = 301.7 kPa

⚠️ Common mistakes in manometer questions

  • Using SG directly as density. Always multiply by 1000.
  • Mixing Pa, kPa, and kN/m² in one line. Pick kPa and stick to it.
  • Forgetting that atmospheric pressure must be added if the question asks for absolute pressure.
  • Sign errors when the tube goes up then down then up. Walk it slowly, one segment at a time.

CH 3Hydrostatic Force & Buoyancy

Two families of question: (1) force on a submerged plane surface, (2) floating/submerged bodies.

Core formulas — submerged plane surfaces

Resultant force
FR = ρ g hc A = γ hc A
Centre of pressure
hp = hc + Ic / (hc A)
Rectangle Ic
Ic = b d³ / 12
Circle Ic
Ic = πR⁴/4 = πD⁴/64
Triangle Ic
Ic = b h³ / 36
Depth to centroid
hc = depth from free surface to the centre of the area
Key insight: hc is the depth to the centroid, NOT to the top or bottom of the plate. And hp is always below hc — if your answer isn't, you made an error.

Core formulas — buoyancy

Buoyant force
FB = ρfluid g Vdisplaced
Apparent weight
Wapp = W − FB
Floating body
W = FB (equilibrium)
Tension when immersed
T = W − FB

Worked Example 1 — Vertical circular plate

A circular surface of diameter 2 m is vertical, with its top edge 0.5 m below the water surface. Find the resultant force and the depth of the centre of pressure.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Depth to centroid (centre of the circle):
    hc = 0.5 + 2/2 = 1.5 m
  2. Area:
    A = πD²/4 = π(2)²/4 = π = 3.1416 m²
  3. Resultant force:
    FR = ρghcA = 1000 × 9.81 × 1.5 × 3.1416 = 46,234 N
  4. Centre of pressure:
    Ic = πR⁴/4 = π(1)⁴/4 = 0.7854 m⁴
    hp = 1.5 + 0.7854/(1.5 × 3.1416) = 1.5 + 0.1667 = 1.667 m
FR = 46.2 kN  ·  hp = 1.667 m

Worked Example 2 — Plywood sheet against concrete (hinge + stop) ⭐

A plywood sheet 1.22 m × 2.44 m retains concrete of depth 0.96 m (SG = 2.4). It is hinged at the top and rests on a stop at the bottom. Find (a) the resultant force and its location, (b) the force on the stop.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Density of concrete:
    ρ = 2.4 × 1000 = 2400 kg/m³
  2. Centroid depth & wetted area:
    hc = 0.96/2 = 0.48 m
    A = 0.96 × 2.44 = 2.342 m²
  3. Resultant force:
    FR = 2400 × 9.81 × 0.48 × 2.342 = 26,470 N
  4. Centre of pressure (rectangle: b = 2.44 m wide, d = 0.96 m tall):
    Ic = (2.44)(0.96)³/12 = 0.180 m⁴
    hp = 0.48 + 0.180/(0.48 × 2.342) = 0.48 + 0.16 = 0.64 m
  5. Force on stop — take moments about the hinge (hinge is at the top, so the lever arm of FR is hp and of Fstop is 0.96 m):
    Fstop × 0.96 = FR × hp
    Fstop = (26.47 × 0.64)/0.96 = 17.65 kN
FR = 26.47 kN at hp = 0.64 m  ·  Fstop = 17.65 kN

Worked Example 3 — Circular window on a cruise ship

A 30-cm-diameter circular window has its midpoint 5 m below the water surface. Seawater SG = 1.025. Find the force on the window and the pressure centre.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Average pressure at centroid:
    Pavg = ρghc = 1025 × 9.81 × 5 = 50,276 N/m²
  2. Area:
    A = π(0.3)²/4 = 0.07069 m²
  3. Force:
    FR = 50,276 × 0.07069 = 3554 N
  4. Centre of pressure — for a circle, hp = yc + R²/(4yc):
    hp = 5 + (0.15)²/(4 × 5) = 5 + 0.0011 = 5.0011 m
FR = 3550 N  ·  hp ≈ 5.00 m (essentially at the centroid)
Why so close? The larger hc is compared to the plate size, the smaller the gap between hc and hp. Deep + small plate → hp ≈ hc.

Worked Example 4 — Crane lowering a steel sphere (buoyancy) ⭐

A 1-m-diameter spherical steel block (ρ = 8050 kg/m³) is lowered into a lake. Find the cable tension when (a) suspended in air, (b) completely immersed.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Volume of sphere:
    V = πD³/6 = π(1)³/6 = 0.5236 m³
  2. Weight in air:
    W = ρsgV = 8050 × 9.81 × 0.5236 = 41,350 N
  3. (a) In air: no buoyancy → T = W = 41.35 kN
  4. (b) Immersed — buoyancy of displaced water:
    FB = ρwgV = 1000 × 9.81 × 0.5236 = 5137 N
    T = W − FB = 41,350 − 5137 = 36,213 N
(a) 41.35 kN  ·  (b) 36.2 kN

Worked Example 5 — Spring scale / apparent weight

A body weighs 7200 N in air and 4790 N when fully submerged in water. Find its volume and density.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Buoyant force = loss of weight:
    FB = 7200 − 4790 = 2410 N
  2. Volume (from FB = ρwgV):
    V = 2410/(1000 × 9.81) = 0.2457 m³
  3. Mass and density:
    m = W/g = 7200/9.81 = 734 kg
    ρ = m/V = 734/0.2457 = 2987 kg/m³
V = 0.2457 m³  ·  ρ = 2987 kg/m³

⚠️ Common mistakes

  • Using the plate's bottom depth instead of the centroid depth.
  • Forgetting to convert cm/mm to m before computing.
  • In buoyancy, using the density of the body instead of the fluid for FB.
  • Using the full height of the object when only part of it is submerged (Vdisplaced is only the submerged volume).
  • For the stop/hinge moment: take moments about the hinge — the lever arm of FR is measured from the hinge, not from the surface.

CH 4Mass Continuity & Bernoulli

Part 1 = flow rates and velocities. Part 2 = pressure/velocity/height trade-offs.

Core formulas

Volume flow rate
Q = A V  (m³/s)
Mass flow rate
ṁ = ρ A V  (kg/s)
Continuity (general)
ρ₁A₁V₁ = ρ₂A₂V₂
Continuity (incompressible)
A₁V₁ = A₂V₂
Bernoulli
P/ρg + V²/2g + z = constant
Torricelli (tank outlet)
V = √(2gh)
Which to use? If the pipe changes size → continuity first, then Bernoulli. If pressures are involved → Bernoulli. If both → continuity to get velocities, then Bernoulli for pressure.

Worked Example 1 — Garden hose (flow rate + nozzle velocity)

A 10-gallon bucket is filled in 50 s. The hose inner diameter is 2 cm and the nozzle exit diameter is 0.8 cm. Find (a) the volume and mass flow rates, (b) the average velocity at the nozzle exit.

1 gal = 3.7854 L · ρwater = 1000 kg/m³

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Volume flow rate:
    Q = (10 gal / 50 s) × 3.7854 L/gal = 0.757 L/s = 7.57 × 10⁻⁴ m³/s
  2. Mass flow rate:
    ṁ = ρQ = 1000 × 7.57 × 10⁻⁴ = 0.757 kg/s
  3. Nozzle exit area (r = 0.4 cm = 0.004 m):
    Ae = π(0.004)² = 5.027 × 10⁻⁵ m²
  4. Exit velocity:
    Ve = Q/Ae = 7.57 × 10⁻⁴ / 5.027 × 10⁻⁵ = 15.1 m/s
(a) Q = 0.757 L/s, ṁ = 0.757 kg/s  ·  (b) V = 15.1 m/s

Worked Example 2 — Pipe fork / branching ⭐

Pipe AB: d = 1.2 m, V = 3 m/s. Pipe BC: d = 1.5 m. At C the pipe forks into CD (d = 0.8 m) which carries 1/3 of the flow in AB, and CE where the velocity is 2.5 m/s. Find (a) QAB, (b) VBC, (c) VCD, (d) dCE.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. (a) Flow in AB:
    AAB = π(1.2)²/4 = 1.131 m²
    QAB = 1.131 × 3 = 3.393 m³/s
  2. (b) Same flow passes through BC:
    ABC = π(1.5)²/4 = 1.767 m²
    VBC = 3.393/1.767 = 1.92 m/s
  3. (c) Branch CD carries 1/3 of QAB:
    QCD = 3.393/3 = 1.131 m³/s
    ACD = π(0.8)²/4 = 0.5027 m²
    VCD = 1.131/0.5027 = 2.25 m/s
  4. (d) Branch CE takes the rest:
    QCE = 3.393 − 1.131 = 2.262 m³/s
    ACE = Q/V = 2.262/2.5 = 0.9048 m²
    dCE = √(4A/π) = √(4 × 0.9048/π) = 1.073 m
(a) 3.393 m³/s  ·  (b) 1.92 m/s  ·  (c) 2.25 m/s  ·  (d) 1.073 m
Branch rule: at a junction, total flow in = total flow out. Write Qin = ΣQout before touching any numbers.

Worked Example 3 — Tank with outlet (Torricelli)

A large tank open to the atmosphere has a water height of 5 m above the outlet tap. Find the velocity at the outlet.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Bernoulli between the free surface (1) and the outlet (2):
    P₁/ρg + V₁²/2g + z₁ = P₂/ρg + V₂²/2g + z₂
  2. Apply conditions: P₁ = P₂ = Patm; V₁ ≈ 0 (large tank); z₁ = 5, z₂ = 0.
  3. The pressure terms cancel and V₁ vanishes:
    z₁ = V₂²/2g
    V₂ = √(2gz₁) = √(2 × 9.81 × 5) = 9.9 m/s
V₂ = 9.9 m/s (Torricelli's equation)

Worked Example 4 — Hose jet height (pressure → height)

Water in a main is at 400 kPa gauge. A hose is held vertically upward. Find the maximum height the jet can reach.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Bernoulli from the hose outlet (1) to the top of the trajectory (2) — at the top, V₂ = 0 and P₂ = Patm.
  2. P₁ here is the gauge pressure, so the atmospheric terms cancel:
    z₂ = P₁,gage / (ρg)
  3. Substitute:
    z₂ = 400,000/(1000 × 9.81) = 40.8 m
z₂ = 40.8 m
Pattern to memorise: gauge pressure ÷ ρg = height in metres. This shows up constantly.

Worked Example 5 — Tapering inclined pipe (continuity + Bernoulli) ⭐

A pipe inclined at 45° converges from d₁ = 200 mm to d₂ = 100 mm over a length of 2 m. Oil (SG = 0.9) flows with V₁ = 2 m/s. Find (a) the pressure difference P₁ − P₂, (b) the manometer reading.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. (a) Continuity gives V₂:
    V₂ = V₁(d₁/d₂)² = 2 × (200/100)² = 8 m/s
  2. Elevation change:
    z₂ − z₁ = 2 sin 45° = 1.414 m
  3. Bernoulli rearranged for pressure difference:
    P₁ − P₂ = ½ρ(V₂² − V₁²) + ρg(z₂ − z₁)
  4. Substitute (ρ = 0.9 × 1000 = 900 kg/m³):
    P₁ − P₂ = 0.5 × 900 × (64 − 4) + 900 × 9.81 × 1.414
    = 27,000 + 12,484 = 39,484 N/m²
  5. (b) Manometer reading (mercury–oil differential):
    h = (P₁ − P₂)/[(ρHg − ρoil)g]
    h = 39,484/[(13600 − 900) × 9.81] = 0.317 m
(a) P₁ − P₂ = 39.5 kPa  ·  (b) h = 0.317 m
Sign watch: if the pipe goes up, z₂ − z₁ is positive and pressure drops extra. If it goes down, the elevation term helps you.

Worked Example 6 — Venturi meter

A Venturi meter has d₁ = 250 mm and d₂ = 100 mm, carrying oil of SG = 0.9. A mercury manometer reads 0.63 m. Find the theoretical volume flow rate.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Areas:
    A₁ = π(0.25)²/4 = 0.0491 m²
    A₂ = π(0.10)²/4 = 0.00785 m²
  2. Venturi equation:
    Q = A₁/√[(A₁/A₂)² − 1] × √[2gh(ρman/ρ − 1)]
  3. Evaluate the area ratio term:
    (A₁/A₂) = 6.25 → (6.25)² − 1 = 38.06 → √38.06 = 6.17
    A₁/6.17 = 0.0491/6.17 = 0.00796
  4. Evaluate the head term:
    2gh = 2 × 9.81 × 0.63 = 12.36
    (13600/900 − 1) = 14.11
    √(12.36 × 14.11) = √174.4 = 13.21
  5. Multiply:
    Q = 0.00796 × 13.21 = 0.105 m³/s
Q ≈ 0.105 m³/s

D1. A rectangular duct 300 mm × 450 mm carries 0.42 m³/s. Find the mean velocity. If the duct tapers to 150 mm × 400 mm, find the new velocity.

Show answer

A₁ = 0.3 × 0.45 = 0.135 m² → V₁ = 0.42/0.135 = 3.11 m/s
A₂ = 0.15 × 0.4 = 0.06 m² → V₂ = 0.42/0.06 = 7.0 m/s

V₁ = 3.11 m/s, V₂ = 7.0 m/s

D2. Air enters a compressor at ρ = 1.2 kg/m³, V = 5 m/s, A = 20 cm². (a) Find ṁ. (b) It leaves through a 25 mm diameter pipe at 4 m/s. Find the exit density.

Show answer

ṁ = ρAV = 1.2 × 20×10⁻⁴ × 5 = 0.012 kg/s
A₂ = π(0.025)²/4 = 4.909 × 10⁻⁴ m²
ρ₂ = ṁ/(A₂V₂) = 0.012/(4.909×10⁻⁴ × 4) = 6.11 kg/m³

(a) 0.012 kg/s  ·  (b) 6.11 kg/m³

D3. A hair dryer: air density at inlet 1.20 kg/m³, at exit 1.05 kg/m³, constant diameter. Find the percent increase in velocity.

Show answer

ρ₁A₁V₁ = ρ₂A₂V₂ and A₁ = A₂ → V₂/V₁ = ρ₁/ρ₂ = 1.20/1.05 = 1.143

14.3% increase in velocity

D4. Air flows at 200 L/s through a pipe that narrows from 20 cm to 10 cm diameter. A water manometer measures the pressure difference. Find the differential height h. (ρair = 1.20 kg/m³)

Show answer

V₁ = 0.2/(π(0.2)²/4) = 6.37 m/s, V₂ = 0.2/(π(0.1)²/4) = 25.5 m/s
P₁ − P₂ = ρair(V₂² − V₁²)/2 = 1.2(650.25 − 40.58)/2 = 365.8 Pa
h = (P₁ − P₂)/(ρwg) = 365.8/(1000 × 9.81) = 0.0373 m

h = 3.7 cm

D5. A pitot-static probe connected to a water manometer gives a deflection of 7.3 cm. Find the air velocity (ρair = 1.25 kg/m³).

Show answer

V = √(2ρwgh/ρair) = √(2 × 1000 × 9.81 × 0.073 / 1.25)
= √(1432.3/1.25) = √1145.8 = 33.8 m/s

V = 33.8 m/s

D6. A closed tank has two inlets and one outlet. Inlet A: d = 150 mm, Q = 56 dm³/s, SG = 0.93. Inlet B: d = 100 mm, Q = 30 dm³/s, SG = 0.87. Outlet C: d = 175 mm. Find the mass flow rate, outlet velocity, and SG of the mixture.

Show answer

ṁA = 930 × 0.056 = 52.08 kg/s
ṁB = 870 × 0.030 = 26.10 kg/s
ṁC = 78.18 kg/s  ·  QC = 0.086 m³/s
VC = 0.086/(π(0.175)²/4) = 3.57 m/s

CH 5Momentum — Jet Striking a Plate ⭐

This is your lecturer's flagged focus. Everything below is narrowed to jets hitting plates and vanes. Master the four cases.

The master equation

ΣF = ṁ (Vout − Vin)   (vector — do it component by component)
Force exerted BY the jet ON the plate = ṁ (Vin − Vout)
Direction discipline: pick a positive direction (say, the jet's original direction). Then write every velocity with its sign. The force you compute is what the plate exerts on the fluid; the jet on the plate is equal and opposite.

The 4 cases you must know

CaseForce normal to plateNotes
1. Stationary flat plate (jet perpendicular) F = ρAV² = ṁV Jet spreads in the plane → zero momentum normal to plate after impact
2. Inclined plate (stationary) F = ρAV² cos θ θ = angle between jet axis and the normal to the plate
3. Moving flat plate (speed u, same direction) F = ρA(V − u)² Use relative velocity. Power P = F × u
4. Curved vane Components: Fx = ṁ(Vin,x − Vout,x), Fy = ṁ(Vin,y − Vout,y) Resultant R = √(Fx² + Fy²), angle φ = tan⁻¹(Fy/Fx)
Angle convention warning: In these notes, θ is measured from the normal to the plate. So θ = 0° → jet hits square-on → maximum force. θ = 90° → jet skims along the plate → zero normal force. If an exam question gives the angle to the plate surface instead, use (90° − that angle).

Worked Example 1 — Jet on an inclined flat plate ⭐⭐

A jet of diameter 25 mm and velocity 5 m/s strikes an inclined flat plate. Find the force normal to the plate as the inclination varies.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Formula for a stationary plate at angle θ from the normal:
    F = ρA(v − u)² cos θ, with u = 0 → F = ρAv² cos θ
  2. Jet area:
    A = π(0.025)²/4 = 4.909 × 10⁻⁴ m²
  3. Reference force ρAv²:
    ρAv² = 1000 × 4.909×10⁻⁴ × 25 = 12.27 N
  4. Evaluate at each angle:
θ (from normal)cos θForce (N)
0°1.00012.27
30°0.86610.63
60°0.5006.14
90°00
Maximum force 12.27 N at θ = 0° (square-on impact); zero at θ = 90°

Worked Example 2 — Curved vane (deflection)

A jet is deflected through 60° by a curved vane. The entry velocity is 30 m/s and the exit velocity is 25 m/s. The mass flow rate is 0.8 kg/s. Find the resultant force on the vane.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Take x along the incoming jet. Inlet: Vin,x = 30, Vin,y = 0.
  2. Outlet: Vout,x = 25 cos 60° = 12.5, Vout,y = 25 sin 60° = 21.65.
  3. x-momentum:
    Fx = ṁ(Vin,x − Vout,x) = 0.8(30 − 12.5) = 14 N
  4. y-momentum:
    Fy = 0.8(0 − 21.65) = −17.32 N
  5. Resultant:
    R = √(14² + 17.32²) = √496 = 22.3 N
  6. Direction:
    φ = tan⁻¹(17.32/14) = 51°
R = 22.3 N at 51° to the horizontal

Worked Example 3 — Stationary vs moving flat plate ⭐

A jet of diameter 50 mm at 18 m/s strikes a flat plate. Find the force when (a) the plate is stationary, (b) the plate moves at 6 m/s in the same direction as the jet.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Jet area:
    A = π(0.05)²/4 = 1.963 × 10⁻³ m²
  2. (a) Stationary (u = 0):
    F = ρAv² = 1000 × 1.963×10⁻³ × 18² = 636 N
  3. (b) Moving at u = 6 m/s — use relative velocity (v − u):
    F = ρA(v − u)² = 1000 × 1.963×10⁻³ × (18 − 6)²
    = 1.963 × 144 = 283 N
(a) 636 N  ·  (b) 283 N
Sanity check: a moving plate always feels less force than a stationary one, because the jet hits it with reduced relative speed. If your moving answer is bigger, you made an error.

Worked Example 4 — Inclined plate, stationary and moving

A jet of d = 25 mm and v = 5 m/s strikes a plate at 30° to the normal. Find the force normal to the plate when (a) the plate is stationary, (b) the plate moves at 2 m/s.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Formula: F = ρA(v − u)² cos θ
  2. (a) Stationary (u = 0):
    F = 1000 × 4.909×10⁻⁴ × 25 × cos 30°
    = 12.27 × 0.866 = 10.63 N
  3. (b) Moving at u = 2 m/s:
    F = 1000 × 4.909×10⁻⁴ × (5 − 2)² × cos 30°
    = 4.909 × 9 × 0.866 = 3.83 N
(a) 10.63 N  ·  (b) 3.83 N

Worked Example 5 — Moving plate with power (braking cart)

A water jet at 15 m/s strikes a cart moving at 5 m/s. The jet's mass flow rate is 25 kg/s. Find the braking force and the power absorbed.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Relative velocity:
    Vrel = 15 − 5 = 10 m/s
  2. Braking force:
    F = ṁ Vrel = 25 × 10 = 250 N
  3. Power absorbed by the cart:
    P = F × Vcart = 250 × 5 = 1250 W
F = 250 N  ·  P = 1.25 kW
Power rule: P = F × u, where u is the speed of the moving body, never the jet speed.

Worked Example 6 — Simple momentum force from mass flow

Water strikes a stationary plate at 20 m/s with a mass flow rate of 10 kg/s. Find the force on the plate.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Perpendicular stationary plate: all the jet's momentum normal to the plate is destroyed.
    F = ṁV = 10 × 20 = 200 N
F = 200 N

Worked Example 7 — Plate on a track (variable force, challenge) 🏆

A 5-cm diameter jet at 18 m/s strikes a plate of mass 1000 kg free to move on a track. Find (a) the acceleration at t = 0, (b) the time to reach 9 m/s, (c) the velocity at 20 s.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Jet area: A = π(0.05)²/4 = 1.963 × 10⁻³ m²
  2. (a) Initial force (plate at rest):
    F₀ = ρAV² = 1000 × 1.963×10⁻³ × 18² = 636 N
    a₀ = F₀/m = 636/1000 = 0.636 m/s²
  3. (b) As the plate speeds up, u rises and force falls:
    m du/dt = ρA(V − u)²
    Integrating from u = 0 at t = 0:
    t = (m/ρA)[ 1/(V − u) − 1/V ]
    For u = 9 m/s:
    t = (1000/1.963)(1/9 − 1/18) = 509.4 × 0.05556 = 28.3 s
  4. (c) At t = 20 s, solve for u:
    1/(18 − u) = 1/18 + (ρA t)/m = 0.05556 + (1.963×10⁻³ × 20)/1 ...
    = 0.05556 + 0.03927 = 0.09483
    18 − u = 10.55 → u = 7.45 m/s
(a) 0.636 m/s²  ·  (b) 28.3 s  ·  (c) 7.45 m/s
Pattern: when the plate's speed changes, the force changes, so you must integrate. The governing relation is m du/dt = ρA(V − u)². If your exam says "find the time", expect this integration.

Bonus — Momentum on pipe bends (secondary, but often paired)

Water flows at 25 kg/s through a 90° elbow of 10 cm diameter with a 35 cm elevation rise. Find (a) the gauge pressure at the inlet, (b) the resultant anchoring force.

Show step-by-step solution
  1. Velocity:
    V = ṁ/(ρA) = 25/(1000 × π(0.1)²/4) = 3.18 m/s
  2. (a) Bernoulli (same velocity magnitude, so velocity terms cancel):
    P1,gage = ρg(z₂ − z₁) = 1000 × 9.81 × 0.35 = 3434 Pa
  3. (b) x-direction momentum (flow turns from +x to +z):
    FRx + P1A₁ = ṁ(0 − V) = −25 × 3.18 = −79.5 N
    P1A₁ = 3434 × 0.007854 = 27.0 N
    FRx = −79.5 − 27.0 = −106.5 N
  4. z-direction:
    FRz = ṁV = 25 × 3.18 = 79.5 N
  5. Resultant:
    FR = √(106.5² + 79.5²) = 133 N
(a) 3.43 kPa  ·  (b) FR ≈ 133 N at ~143° from the positive x-axis

⚠️ Chapter 5 traps

  • Using the absolute jet velocity instead of the relative velocity (V − u) for a moving plate.
  • Using the jet speed instead of the plate speed when computing power.
  • Forgetting that the jet's momentum normal to a flat plate becomes zero after impact — that's why the force is ρAV².
  • Mixing up the angle convention (normal vs. surface).
  • Losing the sign in component problems — always draw the x and y axes and mark directions.
  • Confusing the force of the plate on the fluid with the force of the fluid on the plate (equal and opposite).

✅ Exam-day checklist

  • Write down the given data with units converted to SI first (mm → m, L/s → m³/s, cm² → m²).
  • Draw a sketch. Label the free surface, the datum, and the direction of flow.
  • For manometers: walk the tube, add going down, subtract going up.
  • For hydrostatics: identify hc (to the centroid!) before anything else.
  • For buoyancy: ask "what volume is actually submerged?"
  • For Bernoulli: state your assumptions (steady, incompressible, frictionless, V ≈ 0 at a large surface).
  • For momentum: choose a control volume, draw the axes, and write components separately.
  • For moving plates: always subtract the plate speed from the jet speed before squaring.
  • Carry units through every line — they catch most errors.
  • Check the magnitude: a hose jet should be tens of m/s, a tank outlet a few m/s, hydrostatic forces in kN. Wildly off? Recheck the powers of ten.
